Flip to page 251 of John Maxwell's *Everyone Communicates, Few Connect* and you'll find a wall of names. **Jon Rapusas** is on it. Not because I ghostwrote a chapter or spoke at one of his events. I left comments on his blog when he was still drafting the book online.

That's the part I still like telling people. The manuscript wasn't locked in a publisher's vault. Maxwell posted chapter by chapter on **JohnMaxwellonLeadership.com** for eleven weeks in 2009. Readers could push back, share stories, and argue with the draft while it was still wet. More than a hundred thousand views. Dozens of reader quotes made the final cut. Nearly a hundred edits came from the comment threads. Everyone who showed up in a meaningful way got listed in the back.

I was one of those names. A small line in a leadership bestseller - but it changed how I read the title forever.

---

## Talk is cheap. Connection isn't.

The book's whole argument fits on the cover: **everyone communicates, few connect**.

Maxwell isn't talking about phone signal. He's talking about the moment someone hears your words and actually *lands* with them - extra effort, trust, the feeling that the conversation was worth their time. You know when a call drops on Verizon. He's asking whether you notice when a meeting, a sermon, or a blog post drops the same way.

Part one is five principles. Part two is five practices. Nothing exotic. Connect on common ground. Keep it simple. Make it an experience people enjoy. Inspire action. Live what you say.

What stuck with me wasn't a single chapter. It was the split between **broadcasting** and **bonding**. I can fill a page with correct sentences and still leave you nowhere. Connection is when you leave knowing what to do Monday, or feeling like I actually saw your problem before I offered a fix.

That distinction is why the book aged better than most 2010 business shelves. Social media hadn't finished teaching us that reach and resonance are different animals. Maxwell was already saying it in plain language.

## How I ended up in the back of the book

I don't have a dramatic origin story. I was reading leadership blogs in 2009 the way a lot of us were - hungry for frameworks, trying to get better at teams, clients, and the awkward parts of church volunteer work.

When Maxwell opened the manuscript, I did what the internet exists for: I read, I commented, I moved on with my week. I wasn't angling for a credit line. I didn't even know Maxwell would list everyone's name until the acknowledgments showed up.

Honest admission: I don't remember the exact comment that earned the listing. Maxwell's team pulled more than seventy reader stories into the body text. Mine might have been one of those, or I might have simply been active enough in the threads to make the contributor roll. Either way, the book shipped in 2010 with **Jon Rapusas** in the contributors section, and I bought a copy mostly to verify I wasn't imagining it.

There's a lesson in that process if you build things online. Maxwell treated the blog as a **drafting room**, not a megaphone. He absorbed pushback. He credited people by name. He let strangers improve a book that would sell millions of copies. That's rare even now, when "community input" usually means a poll sticker on Instagram.

## What I still steal for my own writing

I run this site under the same pressure Maxwell describes. Words everywhere. Everyone has a newsletter, a thread, a hot take. The hard part isn't publishing. It's making someone feel like you wrote *to* them, not *at* them.

A few moves from the book show up in how I edit posts here:

**Start on their problem, not my bio.** If the first paragraph is credentials, I've already lost the person who clicked because they're stuck.

**One clear point per section.** Connectors do the hard work of keeping it simple. I still catch myself stacking three ideas in one heading and wonder why the section feels like homework.

**Show the friction.** Connection goes beyond words. A story about the wrong extension cord, a Hugo version mismatch, a burned Instant Pot notice - that's the stuff that tells you a human was in the room.

**End on action, not applause.** Inspire people to do something small the next day. Not "hope this helped." What would *you* try?

I'm not running Maxwell's empire. I'm a freelancer with a Hugo site and a notes garden. The scale is tiny. The test is the same: did this post communicate, or did it connect?

## If you pick it up

*Everyone Communicates, Few Connect* is short, practical, and very John Maxwell - stories, lists, pastor-friendly examples, zero irony. If you hate that style, you'll skim. If you lead teams, teach, preach, sell, or write in public, the framework is worth the afternoon.

Read the contributors page when you're done. Hundreds of names from an experiment in open drafting. Mine's in the R's, right next to people I'll never meet, all linked by the odd fact that we commented on the same manuscript before it was a book.

That's the special thing about this copy on my shelf. It's not autographed. It's **crowdsourced** - and my name is proof I was in the room.
