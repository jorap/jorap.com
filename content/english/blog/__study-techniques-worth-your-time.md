I watched [Justin Sung rank eight popular study techniques](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uf_DUtzHFsU) from F to S in about eighteen minutes. The punchline isn't a new app. It's this: **the methods that feel easy are popular because they're easy - not because they work.** Glad someone ranked them that bluntly.

That matches what I've seen in classrooms and in my own habits. Yellow highlighter across a page. Reread until it feels familiar. Call it studying. Then freeze when someone asks you to use the idea.

Here's what stuck, in plain language.

---

## F tier: highlighting and rereading

Sung puts **highlighting** (or underlining) at F. Decades of research back how little it does for real learning. The only honest use is finding a keyword later when you're already doing harder work.

**Rereading** gets the same grade. More exposure helps a little. Not enough to earn the hours. Familiarity tricks you - you recognize the paragraph, so your brain files it as "known." Recognition is not recall. Recognition is not problem-solving.

His framing on why these stick around is the useful part: low-effort techniques stay popular because they ask almost nothing of your brain. Learning is mostly invisible, so you don't feel the damage until the quiz, the meeting, or the blank page weeks later.

If you throw away the highlighter and stop rereading for "one more pass," you might get better by accident. You just stopped spending time on nothing.

---

## B tier: blurting and flashcards (as most people use them)

**Blurting** (brain dump: close the book, write everything you remember, check gaps, repeat) is real active recall. Sung puts it in B. Free recall makes your brain work. Good.

The ceiling is low. Blurting doesn't force you to ask how ideas connect, which idea matters more, or how one concept changes another. For undergrad fact drills it's okay. For professional work where you decide, explain, or solve, he says it drops hard - almost useless next to better options.

**Flashcards** land in the same band. Cue on one side, answer on the other. Pair them with a spacing algorithm (Anki and friends) and you get active recall plus the spacing effect - that's a strong combo for isolated facts.

Same limit: **isolated facts**. Cards rarely make you weigh ideas against each other or build a schema. Sung's doctor example is the exception that proves the rule - meds and doses you need cold. For most working knowledge you can look up, a giant deck is overkill. Overhyped more than worthless.

I still like cards for a short list of things I need cold when life shows up. I don't pretend a card deck replaces understanding.

---

## C tier: Cornell notes - a starter, not the destination

Cornell splits the page: questions on the left, notes on the right, summary at the bottom. Viral before social media. Sung gives it a C.

Why it helps beginners: writing questions primes your brain for connections. The bottom summary is a mini teach-back. That's deeper than copying the slide verbatim.

Why it stalls: most people write shallow questions ("What is X?") and editorial summaries (long sentence → short sentence) instead of rearranging the idea. The page looks clean, so you get no signal that the thinking was thin. Mind maps, done wrong, at least look messy. Cornell can look finished while still being shallow.

Treat it as a stepping stone - not the system you keep forever.

---

## A and S: Feynman, mind maps, and interleaving - if you do them right

**Feynman technique** gets an A. Learn something, then explain it to a beginner in plain words. Out loud, on paper, whatever. The work is the same: strip jargon, pick what matters, hold a clean story from memory.

Everything blurting and flashcards do well, Feynman does with a higher bar. Sung won't give it a raw S because beginners can't always tell if their own explanation is good. Pair it with expert or AI feedback and he bumps it to S - he dictates to his phone while driving and gets critiqued. [NEEDS SCENE: one time you tried teach-back and stumbled]

**Mind mapping** is the wild swing: D for most people, S when done right. Most folks draw bubbles without changing how they think. Same linear notes, prettier shape. Useless.

Done right, you're comparing ideas, ranking importance, and cleaning a messy big picture on paper so your brain isn't holding the mess alone. That strain is the learning. Sung calls correctly executed mind mapping his personal favorite - and the closest match to how memory actually stores: in connections.

**Interleaving** depends on which definition you got. Classical version (biology thirty minutes, then chemistry, then physics in one sitting) is B - some benefit. Sung's version is S: stay in one topic, but force different angles, different question types, and side-by-side comparison of ideas that are easy to confuse. You're not just recalling. You're finding the edges where one concept ends and another starts.

That last version is the one I'd steal first for anything I actually need to *use*.

---

## The real ranking isn't the technique name

Sung's quiet thesis under the tier list: **you can invent your own methods if you know the principles.** Effective strategies push active work, comparison, and schema building. Ineffective ones skip that - or violate it, like highlighting pretending attention equals understanding.

What I'd actually do after watching:

1. Stop paying for "study" that is only highlight + reread.
2. Keep flashcards for facts I need cold - not for everything.
3. Prefer teach-back and side-by-side comparison over another neat summary page.
4. When a method can be D or S depending on execution, assume I'm currently on the D path until the page feels uncomfortable.

Uncomfortable is the point. If it feels smooth and familiar, you're probably recognizing, not learning. Glad that line stuck.
