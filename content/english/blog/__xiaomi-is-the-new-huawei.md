Open Shopee for a phone charger and you'll scroll past three Xiaomi listings before you find the cable you actually wanted. Walk into a mall phone shop and there's a Redmi Note on the wall next to Samsung and Oppo. Plug a streaming box behind our TV and it's a **Mi Box S** - not because I'm a fanboy, because it was the cheap fix that worked. Glad that cheap fix still works.

That pattern used to be Huawei.

For a few years Huawei felt like the brand that cracked the code: hardware that photographed like a flagship, prices that didn't require selling a kidney, and enough polish that your tito who still prints boarding passes would brag about his Mate. Then the US trade war hit, Google services vanished from new Huawei phones outside China, and the global climb stalled hard.

Xiaomi didn't invent that playbook. It inherited the open lane.

---

## What Huawei was before the sanctions

Huawei wasn't always a phone company in people's heads. It built telecom gear - base stations, carrier contracts, the unglamorous backbone stuff. Phones were the consumer face.

By the late 2010s the consumer face got very good very fast. **P30 Pro** night shots. **Mate** batteries that lasted two days if you squinted. Build quality that made Samsung's midrange feel plasticky. Huawei climbed until it was briefly the **world's second-largest phone seller**, breathing on Samsung's neck in markets where Apple was already the luxury lane.

The trick wasn't one killer spec. It was **flagship feel at midrange money**, plus cameras people could see in side-by-side posts without owning a photography degree.

And the ecosystem creep started early. Laptops, tablets, watches, earbuds, routers - the usual "we live in your house now" expansion. Sound familiar?

---

## What broke

In 2019 the US put Huawei on the entity list. The details are a geopolitics seminar. The consumer result was blunt:

**No Google Mobile Services on new global models.** No Play Store out of the box. No Gmail, Maps, or YouTube the way Android users expect. Huawei pushed **HarmonyOS** and its own app store. Developers and users outside China had to care about sideloading and compatibility in a way nobody should have to explain to their parents.

Chip supply got ugly too. Cutting-edge processors became a chess game. Huawei could still sell phones - especially in China - but the global momentum that made it feel inevitable started leaking out.

I watched friends with older Huaweis hold on as long as they could. When upgrade time came, the conversation wasn't "which Huawei" anymore. It was "okay, what's safe to buy that still runs my apps."

That vacuum didn't stay empty.

---

## How Xiaomi filled the slot

**Lei Jun** has been saying some version of "learn from Apple" for years. People mocked the keynote cosplay. Then Xiaomi kept shipping.

### Phones that show up everywhere

**Redmi** ate the budget floor - Notes and A-series boxes stacked in shops from Manila to Mumbai. **POCO** chases the enthusiast who wants a fast chip and doesn't care about brand prestige - though in the Philippines, a lot of the attractive POCO deals are gray imports without local warranty unless you buy through Xiaomi's official channels. **Xiaomi** proper moved upmarket with numbered flagships and **Ultra** models that exist mostly to win camera comparisons on YouTube.

The pitch is the same one Huawei rode: **spec sheets that embarrass phones twice the price**, cameras good enough for Instagram without a tutorial, and software that's mostly Android with Xiaomi seasoning.

I don't own a Xiaomi phone right now. I don't need to. Half the people in my group chats do, and they all describe the same deal - "it's not perfect, but for what I paid, I'm not complaining."

### The gadget layer I actually live with

Xiaomi's phone story gets the headlines. The gadget story is how it became ambient.

I bought a **Mi Box S** when our TV's built-in OS turned into a slideshow. Small Android TV box, Chromecast built in, tight storage, updates depend on Xiaomi still caring - I wrote the full take in my [Mi Box S post](/blog/mi-box-s/). It's not glamorous. It's **Xiaomi being useful in a room that isn't a phone launch stage**.

Add power banks, robot vacuums, air purifiers, smart bulbs, rice cookers if you're deep in the lifestyle catalog. **Mi Home** ties a lot of it together. Huawei had a similar smart-home push. Xiaomi made the cheap-white-plastic version feel normal in apartments where nobody reads spec forums.

### Cars, because of course cars

Huawei leaned into automotive tech partnerships when phones got harder globally. Xiaomi went further and shipped the **SU7** electric sedan - Lei Jun on stage, internet hype, "Apple Car energy but it actually arrived" jokes.

I haven't driven one. I'm not the target market in the Philippines anyway. But the move rhymes with Huawei's ambition: **don't stay a phone vendor, become the tech layer for a whole life**.

Whether that works long-term is a different essay. The strategic bet is clear. Xiaomi wants the slot Huawei used to own - **the Chinese brand that feels like the future without charging future money**.

---

## What's actually different

Calling Xiaomi "the new Huawei" is useful shorthand. It's not a one-to-one swap.

**Xiaomi still has Google.** Outside China, new Xiaomi phones ship with Play Store, Google Pay where supported, the Android apps people already use. That's the biggest practical gap. Huawei's global phone story broke on software trust as much as hardware. Xiaomi hasn't walked into that trap yet.

**Different roots.** Huawei grew out of carrier infrastructure and enterprise relationships. Xiaomi grew out of online flash sales, forum hype, and razor-thin margins on phones that felt like steals. Huawei could feel corporate even when the camera was great. Xiaomi still feels like a very large startup that also sells air fryers.

**Brand soup.** Huawei was mostly Huawei. Xiaomi is Xiaomi plus Redmi plus POCO plus a dozen sub-brands that confuse normal buyers. Great for market share. Messy when you're trying to recommend one model to a relative who just wants "a good phone."

**Sanctions risk isn't zero.** Any Chinese tech giant doing this well attracts the same political attention Huawei got. I'm not predicting anything. I'm saying the reason Xiaomi can sell a global phone with Gmail preinstalled is partly luck and timing, not a law of nature.

**Software opinions.** **HyperOS** (and MIUI before it) ships features fast and ads in places power users hate. Debloat guides exist for a reason. Huawei's later software was a survival story. Xiaomi's is a "you got a lot for cheap, here's a promotional row" story. Different annoyances.

## What I watch as a buyer

I'm not switching ecosystems over a hot take. I like [Google's glue across my mismatched devices](/blog/google-over-apple-ecosystem/) more than I like any one phone brand. But I notice who wins the "what should I buy" conversations around me.

**For a relative on a budget:** I point at **Redmi** from a Mi Store or Xiaomi's official Shopee store - somewhere the serial number will actually register and you can walk out with a receipt. Samsung's fine if they already trust the brand, but I don't steer people there myself - the A-series still feels expensive for what you get next to a Note on sale.

**For myself:** I skip Samsung on price. I skip **POCO** because I can't buy it the way I buy everything else Xiaomi. Walk into a Mi Store and the wall is Redmi Notes and numbered Xiaomis - power banks, robot vacuums, the usual. No POCO shelf. New POCO launches here tend to land as Shopee or Lazada exclusives anyway, which pushes you back toward gray-import roulette and serial-number warranty checks I'm not doing.

**For the Huawei refugees:** If your last phone was a Mate and you loved the camera but hated losing Google, a **Redmi Note** from a Mi Store or Xiaomi's official Shopee store is the obvious "try this instead" answer. Not identical. Close enough that the conversation moved on.

Huawei taught the market that a Chinese brand could feel premium. Sanctions taught the market that geopolitics can delete your app store. Xiaomi is the company currently running that lesson at scale - phones in every shop, gadgets in every room, an EV on billboards, and still, for now, Gmail when you set the thing up.

That's the new Huawei. Same ambition. Different scars. Cheaper entrance fee. Glad I learned to buy the useful bits without buying the whole identity.
