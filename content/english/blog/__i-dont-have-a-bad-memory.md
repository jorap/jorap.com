I've told myself I have a bad memory more times than I can count. Names at church. A tip from a video I watched the same morning. Something I nodded at in a doc, then blanked when I needed it at the keyboard. Glad I finally stopped treating that as a fixed trait.

I'm not keen on memorizing for its own sake. Rote lists feel like homework I didn't sign up for. What I *am* keen on is learning how learning works - partly for my own work, and partly because I want Davis to inherit better habits than "read it again until it feels familiar."

Then I watched [Justin Sung explain why you don't have a bad memory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UyiKBkUZ2xc). The reframe stuck: **memory is a skill of how you handle information**, not a personality trait you either got or didn't.

That matches the blank moments better than "I'm just forgetful."

---

## The label that lets you off the hook

"Bad memory" is a convenient story. It ends the investigation. You shrug, move on, binge the next thing.

Sung's pitch is blunter. If information never got encoded well, of course you can't pull it back later. That's not a broken hard drive. That's a file that never finished saving.

I do this with learning content all the time. Watch twenty minutes. Feel smart. Close the tab. Next week someone asks me what I learned and I give them the vibe of the video, not the idea.

Familiarity is not recall. I've known that in classrooms. I still fall for it on the couch.

## Working memory is a small workbench

Sung's useful picture: working memory is a short workbench - something like fifteen to thirty seconds of active space. Stuff sits there only while you mess with it. If you just stare, or pile on more input, the earlier pieces fall off the edge.

So "I forgot" often means "I never did anything with it while it was still on the bench."

That reframes the binge. Another article, another tutorial, another Sung video queued up - the workbench fills, nothing gets shaped, and later you blame the storage.

I'm not trying to become a memory athlete. I don't want a palace of loci for grocery lists. I want the few handling moves that stop good ideas from evaporating before Davis is old enough to need them too.

---

## Encode by changing it, not by staring

Passive repetition feels like work. Highlight. Reread. Nod. Your brain files it as "seen," and you walk away empty.

What Sung keeps pointing at instead: **transform the information while it's still warm.** Make it more complex in a useful way - connect it to something you already know, rephrase it, compare it, put it next to a problem you actually have. Process it now, not "later when I review."

And when the bench gets crowded, pause. Don't add more tabs to prove you're learning. Sit with what's already there until it has a shape.

The offload move I respect most from his framing: **think on paper.** Get the raw pile out of your head so your brain can do the connecting job instead of the holding job. Messy notes that rearrange ideas beat neat notes that only copy the source.

I already like teach-back and short flashcard lists for things I need cold - that's a different post. This one is earlier in the chain. If I never encode, no deck saves me.

---

## Drop one passive habit

If I only change one thing after watching, it's this: stop paying for the feeling of learning with more consumption.

What I'd drop this week: the second half of a video when I already have one idea I haven't written down or tried to use. Pause. Scribble the connection. Or explain it out loud like I'm talking to Davis in five years - plain words, no jargon.

What I won't do: build a giant memorization system. That fights how I actually live. Handling first. Storage tricks later, and only for the short list that must be cold.

Retrieval matters too. Sung's point lands for me as a parent and a builder: practice pulling the idea in the situation you'll need it - explaining, deciding, shipping - not only recognizing it on a page you already saw. Recognition is cheap. Use is the test.

---

## What I'd teach Davis first

He's six. He doesn't need a lecture on working memory. He needs adults who don't model "watch more until it clicks."

What I'd pass on, in kid language:

1. **If it felt easy, you might only have recognized it.** Try to say it back without looking.
2. **Write the messy version.** Paper or tablet - get it out of your head so you can play with it.
3. **Stop before you're full.** One solid idea beaten up on paper beats five videos half-absorbed.
4. **Connect it to something you already care about.** Games, stories, a problem he hit today - nearest neighbor, not floating facts.

I still have to practice this myself. The point of watching Sung isn't to collect another technique name. It's to stop calling the empty workbench a character flaw - and to have something better ready when Davis hits real school pressure.

I'm not memorizing for sport. Glad I'm learning how learning works so the next generation in this house doesn't inherit my shrug.
